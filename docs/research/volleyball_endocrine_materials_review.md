# From Surface Chemistry to Athlete Exposure

## A materials-engineering framework for evaluating endocrine-active chemicals in indoor volleyballs

**Status:** Research review and engineering hypothesis. No commercial
volleyball is asserted to cause endocrine disruption, and no listed chemical
is assumed to be present in a ball without direct product testing.

### Abstract

Women's-sport health research has expanded rapidly, but the strongest evidence
base still concerns athlete physiology rather than equipment chemistry.
Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs), menstrual symptoms, iron
deficiency, bone health, sleep, and pelvic-floor dysfunction can influence
health, availability, and performance in female athletes. Volleyball-specific
evidence now exists for menstrual symptoms, perceived performance variation,
and urinary incontinence, but the literature remains heterogeneous and often
relies on small samples or self-report. In parallel, materials and toxicology
literature establishes that selected phthalates, bisphenols, alkylphenols, and
other additives used in plastics, rubber, coatings, and adhesives can have
endocrine or reproductive relevance, and that dermal transfer from consumer or
sports-material articles can occur under some conditions. What is currently
missing is the bridge between those literatures: direct chemical
characterization, migration testing, and exposure modeling for repeatedly
handled volleyballs.

This paper defines that bridge as an engineering problem rather than a
clinical claim. A competition volleyball is a layered, hand-contact article
whose publicly disclosed construction may include microfiber or polymeric
covers, nylon winding, and other elastomeric or adhesive systems. Public
product descriptions do not disclose a complete additive inventory. Therefore
hazard lists, polymer names, or generic regulatory warnings cannot establish
exposure or risk. A defensible program must proceed through evidence gates:
identify materials; quantify targeted chemical content; measure migration into
synthetic sweat; characterize realistic hand contact; quantify dermal uptake
only when warranted; place any dose estimate into chemical-specific
toxicological context; and verify that a lower-migration material preserves
competition performance and durability. This creates a rigorous materials
science research program for BALL 001 even if the final result is that
exposure is negligible.

## 1. Women's health and sports performance: the evidence that already exists

The most defensible starting point is athlete physiology, not equipment
chemistry. The 2023 International Olympic Committee consensus statement on
Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport describes REDs as impaired physiological
or psychological function associated with problematic low energy
availability. The affected systems can include reproductive function,
musculoskeletal health, metabolism, immunity, glycogen synthesis, and
cardiovascular and haematological health, with consequences for injury risk,
training availability, and performance. This is a multi-system syndrome and
is not unique to women, but menstrual and reproductive outcomes are
particularly salient in female athletes.

The menstrual-cycle literature is more nuanced than the claim that one phase
is categorically "better" for performance. A 2020 systematic review and
meta-analysis including 78 studies found only a trivial average reduction in
performance during the early follicular phase, substantial between-study
variation, and low overall evidence quality. A 2026 meta-analysis using
stricter hormonal phase confirmation found no statistically significant
difference in VO2 max between the phases compared and again emphasized the
need for larger, better-standardized elite-athlete studies. The appropriate
engineering and sport-science response is therefore individualized
longitudinal measurement, not a universal cycle-based training prescription.

Volleyball-specific evidence is now emerging. A 2024 study followed 20
professional female volleyball players during a German national-league season
and reported substantial individual variation in menstrual symptoms; stomach
cramps, sleep disturbance, and tiredness were among the most common symptoms.
A 2026 survey of 84 recreational and competitive volleyball players reported
phase-related differences in **perceived** performance and motivation, with
75% of participants reporting menstrual disorders and 59.5% reporting
dysmenorrhea. These are meaningful athlete-welfare data, but they are
subjective and should not be treated as proof that endocrine fluctuations
universally determine objective match performance. A 2025 study in 13 elite
Tunisian players reported associations between menstrual phase, time of day,
and selected physical and psychological outcomes; the small sample makes it
hypothesis-generating rather than a basis for broad policy.

Other women's-health issues matter in volleyball even when they are not
endocrine disorders. A recent systematic review of high-level female athletes
found that iron deficiency can impair endurance performance when deficiency is
present. Pelvic-floor dysfunction is directly relevant to a high-impact,
jumping sport: systematic reviews report elevated urinary-incontinence
prevalence in female athletes, and volleyball-specific competition studies
have reported frequent symptoms and objectively measured urine loss. These
topics belong in a women's-athlete health evidence map because they influence
availability and performance, but they must remain analytically distinct from
chemical exposure.

The first research principle follows from this evidence: **women's health
cannot be collapsed into a single hormone variable.** REDs, menstrual
symptoms, iron status, pelvic-floor function, sleep, contraceptive use,
pregnancy/postpartum status, and injury history are separate domains. None of
them should be used as a surrogate biomarker of exposure from a volleyball.

## 2. Why volleyball-material chemistry is a legitimate question

The materials question is not "is a volleyball toxic?" The scientifically
useful question is narrower: **does repeated hand contact with a layered
polymer article produce measurable transfer of selected chemicals, and can
materials engineering reduce that transfer without degrading the ball?**

Public product descriptions establish plausibility while also exposing the
information gap. Mikasa describes the V200W as an 18-panel competition ball
with a double-dimpled microfiber cover and nylon-wound center. Molten describes
the V5M5000 as a microfiber-composite, nylon-wound competition ball. These
descriptions are useful for product architecture, but they do not identify
plasticizers, catalysts, residual monomers, coating constituents, adhesive
chemistry, pigments, antioxidants, processing aids, or migration rates.

A California Proposition 65 warning is displayed on at least one V200W-USA
product page. That warning cannot be used as evidence that a particular
endocrine-active compound is present, and it certainly cannot be converted
into a dose or health conclusion. It is a prompt for material identification,
not a substitute for it.

Phthalates are a rational screening family because the European Chemicals
Agency states that phthalates can occur in plastics and also in adhesives,
sealants, paints, rubber materials, and sports equipment, and that some
members of the family can interfere with hormonal systems. Reviews of
phthalate exposure and female reproductive health provide biological and
epidemiological reasons to take selected compounds seriously. But those
reviews do not show that any given volleyball contains those chemicals, much
less that handling a ball produces a biologically important dose.

Bisphenols are another conditional screening class. BPA and some analogues can
be endocrine active, but screening is justified only where resin, coating, or
adhesive chemistry makes the class plausible. Alkylphenols may be relevant to
some rubber or surfactant systems. PAHs are primarily a carcinogenic
chemical-safety concern rather than an endocrine endpoint, yet a materials
health program should not ignore a major co-hazard merely because it falls
outside the original hypothesis. Residual isocyanates may warrant testing if
polyurethane chemistry is confirmed, principally as a sensitization and
chemical-safety issue.

This is the essence of a formulation-led screen: **material identity narrows
the chemical question.** An unlimited "test everything" panel is expensive,
statistically noisy, and scientifically weaker than targeted analysis tied to
a known polymer/additive system.

## 3. Presence is not exposure, and exposure is not risk

The most important materials-health distinction is the separation of hazard,
presence, migration, transfer, absorption, and risk context.

A chemical may be present in a polymer yet remain strongly bound or trapped
within the matrix. A chemical may migrate from a surface but only under
conditions unlike actual use. A chemical may reach the skin but cross the
stratum corneum poorly. A measurable absorbed dose may still be small relative
to an appropriate reference value. Each step changes the meaning of the
evidence.

Sports-material analogues show why this distinction matters. Studies of
synthetic-turf rubber have identified PAHs, phthalates, bisphenol A,
4-tert-octylphenol, and other chemicals in material or migration experiments.
One European migration study found several substances in sweat simulant and
used the measured migration rates to inform dermal exposure assessment.
Separate risk assessments have concluded that estimated exposure under studied
conditions was not necessarily at a level of concern. These findings do not
transfer numerically to a volleyball. They demonstrate the correct method:
**measure migration from the actual article under use-relevant conditions
before discussing exposure.**

Volleyball has a further modeling challenge: hand contact is intermittent. A
two-hour practice is not two continuous hours of skin contact with the ball.
A realistic exposure model must measure or estimate the number of touches,
duration per touch, cumulative contact time, effective hand area, moisture
state, surface temperature, contact pressure, practice frequency, and ball
age. Setter drills may provide an especially useful high-contact test case
because touch count can be high and the interaction is repeatable enough for
high-speed-video analysis.

## 4. A seven-gate evidence architecture

BALL 001 should use barrier gates that prevent a weak upstream observation
from becoming a downstream health claim.

### G0 — Material identity

Identify the layers and the athlete-contact surface. Start with supplier
documents where available, then use ATR-FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, optical
microscopy, micro-CT, DSC/TGA, and selective XRF as needed. The output is a
layer map with analytical confidence, not a guessed bill of materials.

### G1 — Total content

Quantify target chemicals in each relevant layer using a validated method.
Likely methods include GC-MS or GC-MS/MS for phthalates and PAHs and LC-MS/MS
for bisphenols or selected alkylphenols. Every result must carry a method,
sample ID, production lot, LOD, LOQ, calibration information, blank result,
spike recovery, and duplicate information. A non-detect is not zero; it is
"not detected above this method's limit."

### G2 — Sweat migration

Place the actual product or coupon into a controlled skin-relevant migration
system. Use synthetic sweat, controlled surface area, contact pressure,
temperature, and duration. Test new and aged/worn surfaces. At least three
replicates should be used per condition, with procedural blanks, matrix
spikes, and duplicate controls. Report migration as mass per unit area and,
when time-resolved, mass per unit area per hour.

### G3 — Realistic transfer

Measure volleyball-specific use rather than borrowing exposure duration from
another product category. High-speed video can estimate contact duration per
touch; instrumented drills can provide touch count, contact area, sweat state,
and force/pressure proxies. Build both a realistic scenario and a deliberately
conservative bound.

### G4 — Dermal uptake

Only escalate if migration is measurable and potentially decision-relevant.
Prefer chemical-specific, matrix-relevant published absorption data. Where
that is inadequate and the question justifies the cost, OECD Test Guideline
428 provides a framework for in-vitro skin absorption using diffusion cells.
The model must preserve four different quantities: mass leaving the article,
mass reaching the skin surface, mass crossing the skin, and estimated systemic
dose.

### G5 — Dose context

Only after an absorbed-dose estimate exists should a qualified toxicologist or
exposure scientist identify an appropriate chemical-specific reference point,
including route, population, endpoint, and uncertainty assumptions. A simple
dose/reference ratio may be useful as a screening metric, but a ratio below
one is not proof of "zero risk" because aggregate exposure and mixture effects
may remain uncertain.

### G6 — Engineering equivalence

A lower-migration material is not a successful volleyball material if it
changes the game. Any substitution must be re-tested for mass, circumference,
pressure retention, compression force-displacement response, hysteresis,
impact/rebound response, dry and wet friction, abrasion, seam/coating
integrity, aging drift, and eventually player-contact feel.

This last gate makes the research genuinely materials-engineering rather than
a product-label exercise.

## 5. Safer-by-design engineering strategies

The strongest interventions act upstream, before exposure has to be managed.

### 5.1 Formulation control and substitution

Create a restricted-substance specification for intentionally added
plasticizers and other additives. Require supplier disclosure of the polymer
grade, plasticizer family, major coating/binder chemistry, pigments, and
change-control events. Where a target chemical is present and decision
relevant, compare lower-migration alternatives.

"Phthalate-free" or "BPA-free" should not be accepted as sufficient design
evidence. Regrettable substitution is possible: replacing a known compound
with a poorly characterized analogue can move the problem rather than solve
it. The replacement chemistry must be identified and screened on its own
merits.

### 5.2 Diffusion-barrier engineering

If a structural material is mechanically excellent but contains a mobile
additive, a thin barrier layer may reduce transport to the surface. This
becomes a coupled diffusion-mechanics problem. Relevant variables include
diffusivity, partition coefficient, coating thickness, adhesion, strain to
failure, abrasion resistance, hydrolysis, and crack formation under repeated
ball deformation. A barrier that works on an unstressed coupon but cracks
after impact cycling is not a valid exposure control.

### 5.3 Surface chemistry

The athlete interacts with the surface, not the bill of materials in the
abstract. Surface energy, porosity, roughness, moisture uptake, and sweat
chemistry can influence chemical transfer and friction simultaneously.
Anti-sweat and grip technologies should therefore be characterized for both
tribology and extractables/migration.

### 5.4 Aging and wear

New-product chemistry is only the first state. UV, ozone, heat, humidity,
cleaners, hand oils, sweat, abrasion, and repeated flexure can alter a polymer
surface or expose lower layers. Migration testing should be repeated after a
defined aging sequence. A "safer-by-design" statement should survive
representative end-of-life conditions, not merely factory release.

### 5.5 Recycled content and feedstock control

Circularity and material health can conflict if recycled feedstock has poorly
controlled additive history. Recycled material should therefore be segregated
by source and polymer family, lot-traceable, and tested for decision-relevant
contaminants. The goal is not to reject recycled content; it is to prevent
circularity claims from outrunning chemical control.

### 5.6 Multi-objective design optimization

Chemical migration becomes one response variable among several. A structured
DOE can vary polymer family, plasticizer system, coating chemistry, recycled
fraction, texture, and barrier thickness while measuring migration, grip,
compression, pressure retention, mass, abrasion, and durability. The result
should be a Pareto frontier, not a single "green" score. The preferred design
is the least problematic chemistry that still satisfies the performance
envelope.

## 6. Why center women athletes without overclaiming

The rationale is not that women are uniquely harmed by touching a volleyball.
The defensible rationale is that several health endpoints of interest are
sex-specific, women's sport has a growing need for sport-specific research,
and volleyball offers a repeated hand-contact use case in which women comprise
a major competitive population.

That framing requires methodological discipline. Athlete physiology and
product chemistry should run as parallel research streams. Menstrual symptoms,
REDs screening, iron status, pelvic-floor function, contraceptive use, or
performance variation should **never** be used as evidence that a ball caused
an endocrine effect.

A later biomonitoring study would be a completely different stage. Measuring
urinary or blood biomarkers before and after ball use would require
human-subject ethics review, informed consent, privacy controls, pre-registered
hypotheses, appropriate control exposures, and careful accounting for food,
cosmetics, medical products, indoor dust, occupational exposure, and other
sources. That complexity is exactly why biomonitoring is not the first
experiment. The first experiment is the ball.

## 7. Proposed minimum viable study

A high-information initial study can be done without recruiting human subjects
for biomonitoring.

1. Purchase one new ball from each of two elite competition models, ideally
   expanding to multiple lots when budget allows.
2. Prepare one or more BALL 001 outer-surface coupons with complete supplier
   provenance.
3. Map the surface and layer polymers using ATR-FTIR plus microscopy; use
   Raman/thermal methods only when needed.
4. Run a targeted outer-layer screen for phthalates, bisphenols, and PAHs,
   adding other targets only when chemistry justifies them.
5. Run synthetic-sweat migration under one realistic and one conservative
   condition, with at least triplicate samples and full laboratory QA/QC.
6. Use high-speed video of a setter drill to estimate cumulative hand-contact
   time and a plausible effective area.
7. Calculate external dermal load using **measured product migration only**.
8. Repeat migration after controlled abrasion/aging.
9. If a target is measurable and materially important, escalate to
   chemical-specific dermal uptake and toxicology review.
10. Build a lower-migration coupon and re-run both chemistry and BALL 001
    mechanical acceptance tests.

A well-supported null result is useful. If migration is below quantification
limits under realistic and conservative conditions, that replaces speculation
with an evidence-bounded finding. If migration is measurable, it creates a
specific engineering variable that can be reduced.

## 8. Limitations and interpretation rules

The strongest current conclusion is a **research gap**, not a hazard
conclusion. There is credible evidence that some endocrine-active chemicals
are used in consumer plastics, rubber, coatings, and adhesives. There is
credible evidence that selected compounds can migrate from some consumer and
sports materials. There is credible evidence that female-athlete health and
performance are affected by endocrine, energy-availability, iron, pelvic-floor
and other factors. This review did not identify direct evidence that routine
indoor-volleyball handling produces a clinically meaningful endocrine dose.

Three interpretation rules are therefore mandatory.

First, chemical presence does not prove exposure or risk. Second, a
non-detect does not prove absolute absence; it is conditional on method
sensitivity. Third, replacing a named chemical with an uncharacterized
alternative is not automatically safer.

The project should also resist causal overreach. If an athlete reports fatigue,
cycle symptoms, injury, or altered performance, the correct response is not to
assign those outcomes to equipment chemistry. Product exposure would have to
be independently measured and then investigated with an appropriately
designed human study.

---

## Conclusion

Volleyball offers a credible white-space problem at the intersection of
women's sports, materials science, exposure engineering, and product design.
The immediate question is not whether volleyballs cause endocrine disruption.
It is whether a high-contact sports article can be chemically characterized
well enough that material health becomes an explicit engineering requirement
rather than an assumption.

The pathway is measurable: identify materials, quantify targeted chemistry,
measure sweat migration, characterize realistic hand contact, escalate to
dermal uptake only where justified, interpret dose with qualified toxicology,
and validate any substitution against the mechanical performance athletes
trust. That framework remains valuable whether the measured exposure is high,
low, or non-detectable.

For BALL 001, the most defensible contribution is not an endocrine-risk claim.
It is a reproducible way to ask the question correctly and then engineer
against what the measurements actually show.
