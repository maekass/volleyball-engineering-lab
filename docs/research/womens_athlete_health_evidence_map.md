# Women's Athlete Health and Volleyball Performance Evidence Map

**Scope:** research map for Volley Lab / BALL 001. This file separates
athlete-health evidence from product-material exposure evidence. It is not
medical guidance and should not be used to infer that equipment chemistry
explains an athlete's symptoms.

## Evidence architecture

The project uses two parallel evidence tracks.

### Track A — athlete physiology and performance

These questions concern the athlete regardless of ball chemistry: energy
availability, menstrual symptoms, iron status, pelvic-floor function, sleep,
contraceptive status, recovery, and other determinants of availability and
performance.

### Track B — product chemistry and exposure

These questions concern the volleyball: material identity, additive content,
migration into sweat, hand transfer, dermal uptake, dose context, and
lower-migration design alternatives.

The tracks may be connected only after a product-specific exposure pathway is
measured. Menstrual symptoms, REDs, iron status, or performance variation must
not be treated as biomarkers of volleyball-material exposure.

## Current evidence map

| Topic | What current evidence supports | Volleyball-specific status | Project implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) | Problematic low energy availability can affect reproductive, musculoskeletal, metabolic, immune, cardiovascular/haematological and performance outcomes | Not unique to volleyball; relevant to female team-sport athletes | Athlete-health covariate; never attribute to equipment without exposure evidence |
| Menstrual cycle and objective performance | Population-average effects are generally small/inconsistent; individual responses can matter | Emerging volleyball studies exist, but several are small or rely on subjective outcomes | Use individual longitudinal tracking; avoid universal phase rules |
| Menstrual symptoms | Symptoms can affect readiness, sleep, fatigue and perceived performance | Professional volleyball tracking shows cramps, sleep disturbance and tiredness are common | Consider symptom burden when studying contact behavior or performance |
| Iron deficiency | Common in high-level female athletes and can impair endurance when deficiency is present | Not volleyball-specific | Relevant health/performance covariate; clinical management belongs with qualified care teams |
| Pelvic-floor dysfunction / urinary incontinence | High-impact female athletes have elevated symptom prevalence in several reviews | Volleyball is repeatedly represented as a high-impact modality; competition studies report frequent symptoms | Important volleyball-specific women's-health research area, but separate from endocrine-material exposure |
| Hormonal contraception | Effects on performance are heterogeneous and individual | Frequently present in female-athlete cohorts | Record in future physiology studies; not a proxy for chemical exposure |
| Endocrine-active material additives | Some phthalates/bisphenols can affect endocrine or reproductive pathways; use depends on formulation | No direct volleyball-specific exposure study identified in this review | Measure the ball before discussing exposure or risk |
| Dermal transfer from sports materials | Migration into sweat simulants has been measured for some rubber sports-material analogues | No direct elite-volleyball migration dataset identified | Build a volleyball-specific migration and contact protocol |

## High-confidence athlete-health findings

### REDs and low energy availability

The 2023 IOC REDs consensus is the strongest organizing framework for
energy-availability-related health and performance risk. It emphasizes that
the physiological response is multi-system and individually moderated. For
Volley Lab, REDs belongs in the athlete-health evidence layer, not in the
materials-exposure model.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/

### Menstrual cycle and performance

The 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis of 78 studies found only a
trivial pooled reduction in performance during the early follicular phase and
judged the evidence quality low. A 2026 meta-analysis using stricter hormonal
phase confirmation found no statistically significant VO2 max differences
across the phases compared, while emphasizing larger and better standardized
elite-athlete studies.

Sources:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32661839/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42455790/

### Volleyball-specific menstrual evidence

A 2024 study followed 20 professional female volleyball athletes through a
German league season. Commonly reported symptoms included stomach cramps,
sleep disturbance, and tiredness, with substantial athlete-to-athlete
variation.

A 2026 survey of 84 recreational and competitive volleyball players reported
phase-related differences in **perceived** performance and motivation; 75%
reported menstrual disorders and 59.5% reported dysmenorrhea. These are
self-reported outcomes and should not be converted into universal
objective-performance rules.

A 2025 study in 13 elite Tunisian players reported menstrual-phase and
circadian associations with selected performance, mood, and sleep outcomes.
The very small sample makes this useful for hypothesis generation rather than
broad policy.

Sources:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39005625/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42347465/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40176188/

### Iron status

A recent systematic review of 23 studies involving 669 high-level female
athletes found that iron deficiency can impair endurance performance and that
correction of deficiency can improve performance. The finding supports
screening and clinical management in appropriate settings; it is not a basis
for unsupervised supplementation.

Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39536912/

### Pelvic floor

Pelvic-floor dysfunction is especially relevant to a jumping sport.
Systematic reviews report urinary incontinence across female athletes and
higher prevalence in high-impact disciplines. Volleyball-specific competition
research also reports substantial self-reported and objectively measured urine
loss in both amateur and professional players.

Sources:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32774559/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32991079/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29552736/

## Material-health white space

A search of the current literature found strong evidence for
endocrine/reproductive relevance of selected chemical classes and strong
precedent for migration testing in polymer/rubber articles, but did **not**
identify a direct study demonstrating clinically meaningful endocrine exposure
from routine indoor-volleyball handling.

That absence should be treated as a measurement gap, not as evidence of either
safety or harm.

Relevant source categories include:

- ECHA identifies phthalates as a chemical family used in plastics and also in
  adhesives, sealants, paints, rubber materials and sports equipment; some
  members can interfere with hormonal systems:
  https://echa.europa.eu/hot-topics/phthalates
- Reviews link selected phthalate exposures with female reproductive endpoints,
  while emphasizing uncertainty and dose context:
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35248714/
- Synthetic-turf studies show why migration matters: some chemicals present in
  rubber can migrate into sweat simulant, but product presence alone does not
  establish athlete risk:
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32092510/
- OECD TG 428 provides an accepted framework for in-vitro dermal absorption
  when escalation beyond migration is justified:
  https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/test-no-428-skin-absorption-in-vitro-method_9789264071087-en.html

## BALL 001 research rule

The physiology track and materials track should be joined only through measured
exposure evidence:

**material identity → total content → sweat migration → realistic hand transfer
→ dermal uptake → dose context → engineering substitution → performance
equivalence**

A product-health claim cannot skip this chain.
